What an executive agreement should help you understand

An executive agreement is not only a record of compensation. It is a map of the relationship before the role begins, while authority is being exercised and after the relationship ends. A useful review should turn that map into a short list of decisions, risks and questions.

Start with the whole arrangement

The signed agreement may not contain every important term. Compensation plans, equity documents, bonus policies, offer letters, confidentiality agreements, restrictive covenants and company policies may be incorporated by reference or delivered separately. Before evaluating a clause, identify every document that controls the relationship, who can change each document and what happens when the documents conflict.

This first step can reveal practical gaps. A bonus may be described in one document while eligibility, discretion and payment timing appear in another. Equity may be promised in an offer but governed by a plan and a separate award agreement. A role may carry responsibilities that never appear in the written terms. The review should make those relationships visible.

Understand how value is earned, measured and lost

Base compensation is usually the simplest term. Incentives may depend on revenue, performance measures, board discretion, continued employment, a transaction closing or the payment date. Equity may be subject to vesting, repurchase, forfeiture, tax elections or separate plan rules. Deferred compensation and benefits may have their own eligibility and payment conditions.

A useful review separates the headline value from the conditions attached to it. Ask how performance is measured, who decides whether a target was met, when a payment becomes earned, what information will be available to verify the calculation and what happens if employment ends between performance and payment.

Define authority as carefully as responsibility

Titles can stay the same while decision rights, reporting lines, budget authority or resources change. Executives should understand who they report to, what they control, what requires board or investor approval and whether the organization may materially change the role or location.

These provisions matter because an executive may be judged on outcomes without having clearly defined authority to produce them. They can also affect whether a later change triggers notice, negotiation or a defined termination right. The agreement should be read alongside the actual operating structure, not as an isolated document.

Read every possible ending

The consequences may differ if the executive resigns, is terminated without cause, is terminated for cause, leaves for a defined good reason, becomes disabled, dies or remains through a change in control. Each path may change severance, bonus treatment, equity, benefits, notice and transition duties.

Definitions deserve close attention. A broad cause definition can place compensation or equity at risk. A narrow good-reason process may require prompt written notice and an opportunity for the company to cure the issue. A change-in-control benefit may depend on both a transaction and a later termination. The practical question is not simply what the agreement promises, but what steps preserve the promise.

Look beyond the last day

Post-employment terms may affect where an executive can work, whom the executive can contact, what information can be used, how the departure is described and whether cooperation is required. Confidentiality, nonsolicitation, noncompetition, invention-assignment, return-of-property and nondisparagement terms should be read together.

The review should also address transition logistics. Who announces the departure? What happens to company devices and personal files? Does the executive retain access to records needed for taxes or benefits? Are references, public statements or regulatory disclosures anticipated? These issues may be easier to resolve before a dispute or deadline changes the tone.

Documents to gather after conflicts are cleared

  • The offer letter, employment agreement and every amendment
  • Bonus, commission, equity and deferred-compensation documents
  • Confidentiality, restrictive-covenant and intellectual-property agreements
  • Relevant policies, board approvals and written descriptions of authority
  • Prior agreements that may still impose obligations
  • The decision deadline and the two or three outcomes that matter most

Questions a practical review should answer

  • Which terms are fixed, discretionary or controlled by another document?
  • What compensation is earned now, and what remains conditional?
  • What changes can the company make without the executive's consent?
  • What notice or process applies before either side ends the relationship?
  • Which obligations continue after employment, and how do they interact?
  • Which issues are consequential enough to negotiate before signing?

Use timing to improve the decision

Review is most useful before an offer is accepted, an amendment is signed or a departure is announced. If a decision is already moving, preserve the current documents and identify the actual response date. Do not assume that a conversation, draft exchange or request for more time changes a written deadline.

A practical review does not attempt to rewrite every sentence. It ranks terms by consequence, likelihood and negotiability, then explains the tradeoff attached to each proposed change. The goal is a prioritized decision rather than a pile of comments.
