Before discipline or separation: an employer decision map

Employment decisions become harder when urgency replaces sequence. A useful pause is not delay for its own sake. It is time used to identify the actual decision, test the supporting facts and plan an execution that matches the reasoning.

Working draft updated August 2026Attorney review required before publication

1. Define the decision before gathering more facts

Deciding whether conduct occurred, whether a policy applies, whether discipline is warranted and whether employment should continue are different questions. Mixing them can turn an investigation into an argument for an outcome that has already been chosen.

Write the decision in one sentence. Identify who owns it, when it must be made and what information would materially change the answer. This helps the organization avoid collecting every available fact while missing the few facts that matter.

2. Identify legal and contractual overlays early

Protected complaints, leave, accommodation requests, wage concerns, safety reports, contracts, collective-bargaining obligations and prior representations may change the analysis. Timing can also create an appearance of retaliation even when the underlying performance concern is legitimate.

The purpose of this review is not to assume that every workplace issue is legally protected. It is to identify questions that should be answered before a final decision. The analysis should distinguish the employee's conduct or performance from any protected activity and explain how each affected the outcome.

3. Decide what fact development is actually needed

Some decisions can be made from established records. Others require interviews, document review, technical analysis or an independent investigator. The scope should match the question, the seriousness of the allegation and the people involved.

Before starting interviews, identify the allegations, potential witnesses, relevant records and the person who will receive the findings. Decide whether the investigator will make factual findings, credibility assessments, recommendations or some narrower report. A clear assignment reduces drift and inconsistent expectations.

4. Compare the right examples

Consistency matters, but similarity may depend on role, decision-maker, policy, history, timing, knowledge and seriousness. A useful comparator review explains why earlier examples are comparable or why they are not. It should not rely on a spreadsheet of outcomes without the surrounding facts.

Look for patterns in both directions. Has the organization treated similar conduct differently? Has it applied a policy more strictly after a change in leadership? Has the employee received expectations or coaching that others did not? The record should address meaningful differences rather than ignoring them.

5. Test the policy and the actual practice

A written policy may reserve discretion, but the organization's actual practice can shape employee expectations and later explanations. Confirm which version of the policy applied, whether it was distributed, whether managers followed it and whether any required review or approval occurred.

If the decision departs from ordinary practice, identify the reason. The explanation may be sound, but it should be understood before the meeting and reflected consistently in the decision record.

6. Separate the decision record from advocacy

The record should show the facts relied on, the policy or expectation involved, the decision-maker, the alternatives considered and the reason for the outcome. It should be accurate, specific and consistent with what the organization will communicate.

A long document is not automatically a stronger document. Avoid speculation, unnecessary character judgments and legal conclusions unsupported by the decision-maker's role. Preserve underlying records according to the organization's obligations and any litigation hold.

7. Plan the conversation and the first week after

Decide who will speak, what will be provided in writing, what access will change, how property will be returned, how work will be reassigned and what managers may say. Consider final pay, benefits, records, security, customer or patient continuity and internal communications.

A supportable decision can still create avoidable risk if execution is inconsistent or careless. The message should match the actual reasoning. Managers should know what they can communicate and where to route questions.

Documents to gather after conflicts are cleared

  • The applicable policy, agreement and job expectations
  • Relevant performance, attendance or conduct records
  • Prior coaching, discipline and employee responses
  • Complaints, accommodation or leave records that may affect the sequence
  • Comparable decisions with enough context to evaluate similarity
  • The proposed timeline, decision-maker and communication plan

Questions to answer before acting

  • What precise decision is being made, and who has authority to make it?
  • Which facts are established, disputed or still missing?
  • Does protected activity, leave, accommodation or a contract affect the process?
  • How have genuinely similar matters been handled?
  • What explanation will be given, and does the written record support it?
  • What must happen immediately after the decision?

When earlier counsel may help

Earlier review may be useful when senior leadership is involved, protected activity may be present, facts are disputed, a regulator or law-enforcement agency is involved, the employee has counsel, public scrutiny is possible or the organization needs an independent process. Begin with the parties, the general issue and the decision date. Do not send sensitive records until conflicts are cleared.

Working sources for attorney review

The final article should be reviewed against the employer's actual policies and agreements, current guidance from the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, applicable wage-and-hour guidance from the U.S. Department of Labor, and any Virginia-specific authority identified by the reviewing attorney.

This working draft provides general information only and does not create an attorney-client relationship. It does not prescribe an outcome for a particular employee or workplace. Attorney and source review are required before publication.
